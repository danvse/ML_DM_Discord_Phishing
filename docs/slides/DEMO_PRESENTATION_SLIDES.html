Why Discord Scams are So Dangerous

High Trust Between Friends & Rapid Server Spreading

PRESENTER ACTION [SLIDE: Explain the 4 common scam tricks & lateral spreading]
  • Built-in Trust: Discord is made of private servers and friend chats, not public feeds. People naturally trust friends.
  • Account Hijacking: Once an account is hacked, automated bots blast scam links to all their friends and servers.
  • Attack Pretext 1 • Fake Nitro: 'Steam is giving away 3 months of Discord Nitro!'
  • Attack Pretext 2 • Fake Game Tests: 'My friend made an indie game, can you test it?' (installs malware).
  • Attack Pretext 3 • Tournament Voting: 'Vote for my team' links that steal your account login token.
  • Attack Pretext 4 • Crypto Drainers: Fake giveaways designed to empty connected digital wallets.
VISUAL Attack Tactics: Lookalike Letters & Disguised Links
🎙 Presenter Spoken Dialogue (0:45 - 1:30)

On Slide 2, let's look at why Discord scams work so well.



Unlike Twitter or public forums, Discord is built on personal friendships and private gaming servers. When an account gets compromised—often through a fake QR code or malware—automated bots hijack it and immediately message the victim's friends list.



Because the message comes from a close friend you talk to every day, your guard is down.



Attackers usually use four pretexts: free Discord Nitro, asking you to test a new indie game demo, voting for a gaming team, or fake crypto giveaways. Once someone clicks, their account gets compromised too, spreading the infection across the whole server.

How Attackers Easily Bypass Basic Rules & Regular Expressions

  • Bypass 2 • Disguised Links: Using chat formatting like [discord.com](evil.com). The blue text looks official, but clicking opens a scam site.
  • Bypass 3 • Friendly Openers: Starting with 'Hey, are you free?' with no link at all, waiting for a reply first.

Slide 3 shows why basic rules and keyword blocklists fail.



Most Discord servers use AutoMod or moderation bots that look for banned words or specific URLs. But scammers easily get around them using three simple tricks:



First, lookalike letters—technically called homoglyphs. For example, replacing the English letter 'o' with the Russian Cyrillic letter 'о'. To a human, discord.com looks completely normal. But to a rule looking for ASCII English letters, it doesn't match at all, so the scam slips right past!



Second, disguised links: using Markdown to make the link text look like official Discord, while the actual click opens a malicious site.



And third, friendly conversational messages that don't send the link until you reply.



When I tested traditional filters against these tricks, their accuracy plummeted by nearly 40%. We need smart machine learning, not just static rules.

Our Solution: Dual-Stream Machine Learning

Looking at Both the Words and the Structure of the Link

PRESENTER ACTION [SLIDE: Explain the two streams and why XGBoost was chosen]
  • Key Insight: Scammers can rewrite their message in seconds, but to steal your account, they MUST send you to their website!
  • Stream A (The Words): Checks for pushy scam words ('free', 'gift', 'nitro', 'claim').
  • Stream B (The Link Clues): Checks 17 structural hints that don't depend on specific words:
  • • Does the link use foreign lookalike letters?
  • • Is it trying to imitate a famous brand like Discord or Steam?
  • • Does the website domain look like random, messy gibberish?
  • Model Choice: We use XGBoost decision trees. It runs in just 1.2 ms, while heavy neural networks like BERT take 300 ms and cause chat lag.
VISUAL Stream B: 17 Structural Domain Invariants
🎙 Presenter Spoken Dialogue (2:30 - 3:30)

On Slide 4 is my solution: a Dual-Stream Detection Pipeline.



The main insight here is simple: scammers can change their message wording in five seconds, but they cannot hide their malicious website link.



So I built two streams: Stream A looks at the message words: it checks for pushy scam language like 'free', 'claim', and 'nitro'. Stream B looks at the link itself: it extracts 17 structural clues that attackers cannot easily disguise. It checks for lookalike letters, disguised link targets, how close the link is to real brand names, and whether the domain looks like random gibberish.



We feed both streams into XGBoost, a fast tree-based machine learning model.



I chose XGBoost over a heavy deep learning model like BERT for one big reason: speed. BERT takes around 300 milliseconds on a CPU, which would cause server chat queues to back up. XGBoost runs in just 1.2 milliseconds.

Smart Clues: How the Model Catches Fakes

Brand Similarity, Gibberish Scores, and Practice Against Tricks

PRESENTER ACTION [SLIDE: High-level explanation of the 3 detection concepts]
  • Clue 1 • Brand Lookalike Score (Edit Distance):
  • • Measures how many letter changes it takes to turn the link into 'discord.com' or 'steamcommunity.com'.
  • • 'discoord.com' is only 1 letter off—immediate red flag! We use a smart shortcut to keep it super fast.
  • Clue 2 • Gibberish Score (Domain Randomness):
  • • Real company names use smooth, readable words.
  • • Automated scam bots generate random strings like 'x7k9-free-nitro.ru'. Our score catches this messy letter randomness.
  • Clue 3 • Practice Against Sneaky Tricks (Adversarial Training):
  • • During training, we intentionally attacked our own model with disguised links so it learned how to catch them.
VISUAL Detection Clues: Brand Distance, Randomness & Hardening
🎙 Presenter Spoken Dialogue (3:30 - 4:30)

Slide 5 shows the three intuitive ideas that give Stream B its power:



First, a Brand Lookalike Check. We calculate how many letter edits it takes to turn the link into an official brand like discord.com or steamcommunity.com. If a scammer sends discoord.com, that's just a single letter difference, which immediately triggers suspicion.



Second, a Gibberish Score. Real websites have readable, pronounceable names like discord or steam. Automated scam sites often use random scrambled letters, like x7k9-nitro.ru. By measuring how random the letters are, the model spots bot-generated domains right away.



And third, practicing against sneaky tricks. Rather than only training on clean data, we intentionally created synthetic lookalike links during training so the model practiced catching tricky variations.

Now Professor, I want to discuss that 100% result with complete scientific honesty: Whenever you see 100% in machine learning, you have to be skeptical. In my experiment, the model caught all 178 scams because the mutation generator I used for testing was similar to the one used in training. In the open wild, attackers will invent new tricks, so real-world performance will naturally be lower.



To verify that the model wasn't just overfitting, I ran 5-fold cross-validation with randomized seeds. It achieved a very stable 98.8% recall with only a 1% variance, confirming that our link features are genuinely reliable.
